# Business Case Skill

## When to use
- A team needs funding or headcount approval and must justify it in writing
- A technology investment or vendor selection needs a structured recommendation
- A strategic initiative requires a cost-benefit analysis for board approval
- A project is over budget and needs a revised business case to continue
- A "build vs buy" or "make vs outsource" decision needs to be documented

## Business case structure

### Standard business case
1. **Executive summary** — problem statement, proposed solution, total investment required, expected return, recommendation, and decision needed. Maximum one page. Written last.
2. **Problem statement** — what is the business problem, opportunity, or risk being addressed? Quantify the current cost of inaction where possible.
3. **Proposed solution** — what is being proposed? What does it include and exclude? What alternatives were considered and why were they rejected?
4. **Financial analysis**
   - Total cost of investment (capex, opex, one-off, ongoing)
   - Expected benefits (revenue uplift, cost reduction, risk reduction — quantified)
   - NPV, IRR, and payback period (use realistic discount rate and timeframe)
   - Sensitivity analysis: what happens if benefits are 20% lower or costs are 20% higher?
   - Break-even analysis
5. **Non-financial benefits** — strategic alignment, capability building, regulatory compliance, customer experience improvements. Quantify where possible; flag as qualitative where not.
6. **Implementation plan** — key milestones, timeline, resource requirements, and dependencies.
7. **Risks** — top 3–5 risks to the investment delivering the stated benefits. Include likelihood, impact, and mitigation for each.
8. **Recommendation** — explicit statement of what approval is being sought, from whom, and by when.

## Writing rules for business cases
- Never bury the recommendation at the end. The executive summary must contain the full recommendation.
- All financial benefits must be traceable to a specific assumption. State the assumption explicitly.
- Distinguish one-time costs from ongoing costs clearly.
- Include a "do nothing" baseline — what happens if the investment is not made?
- The recommendation must be specific: not "we recommend proceeding" but "we recommend approving $450K capex to implement X by Q3, with a target payback of 18 months."
- Flag dependencies that are outside the control of the proposal owner.

## Financial modelling guidance
- Use a 3-year or 5-year horizon for most business cases (match to asset life or strategic planning cycle)
- Discount rate: use the organisation's WACC or stated hurdle rate
- NPV > 0 and IRR > hurdle rate = financially justified (subject to strategic fit and risk)
- Payback period: flag if > 3 years as this increases execution risk

## Tone
Confident and evidence-based. A business case is an argument for a decision — it should be persuasive but not exaggerated. Every claim must be supportable.

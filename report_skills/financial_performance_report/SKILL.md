# Financial Performance Report Skill

## When to use
- Monthly or quarterly financial results need to be packaged for management or the board
- A business unit needs a P&L commentary for the CFO
- Budget vs actuals analysis needs to be structured into a narrative
- Year-on-year or period-on-period performance needs to be explained, not just presented
- A finance team member asks for help turning numbers into a management commentary

## Report structure

### Standard financial performance report
1. **Performance headline** — one sentence: were results ahead, in line, or behind expectations, and by how much?
2. **Key metrics summary** — revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, net profit, and the primary KPIs for the business. Show actual vs budget vs prior period for each.
3. **Revenue commentary** — volume, price, mix analysis. What drove the result? Which segments outperformed or underperformed?
4. **Cost commentary** — fixed vs variable cost movements. What drove cost changes? Are there one-off items that distort the underlying trend?
5. **Margin analysis** — gross margin and EBITDA margin vs prior periods and budget. Flag structural vs cyclical changes.
6. **Budget variance analysis** — for each material variance (>5% or above materiality threshold), explain: what happened, why, and whether it is expected to continue.
7. **Outlook** — revised expectations for the remainder of the period. Flag any risks to the revised view.
8. **Actions required** — specific decisions or escalations needed from the reader.

## Writing rules for financial reports
- Lead every section with the conclusion, not the data. ("Revenue was $12.4M, 3% below budget, driven by lower-than-expected volume in the enterprise segment" — not a table with no commentary.)
- State variances in absolute dollars and percentage terms.
- Distinguish one-off items from structural trends explicitly.
- Use consistent terminology throughout: do not mix "revenue", "sales", and "income" interchangeably.
- Every material variance must have a cause assigned — "unknown" is not acceptable.
- Do not use weasel language ("somewhat below", "a little ahead"). Quantify everything.
- Flag any restatements or methodology changes clearly.

## Tone
Professional, factual, direct. Written for a reader who knows finance — avoid explaining basic accounting concepts. Focus on the business story behind the numbers.

## Quality rules
- Do not invent figures. If data is missing, state what is needed and leave a placeholder.
- Distinguish facts (actual results) from estimates (budget, forecast) clearly.
- If the user provides numbers with no context, ask for the relevant comparison period and budget before writing.
